1. Purpose
ArticleCompare AI helps editors, SEO specialists, content teams and website owners compare multiple drafts before publication. It evaluates articles against a shared rubric, identifies strengths and weaknesses, highlights content gaps, recommends improvements and produces a defensible editorial recommendation.
It is an editorial decision-support product. It does **not** predict Google/Bing rankings, establish factual truth without evidence, or replace human editorial judgment.

01 — Product Requirements Document (PRD)


1. Product overview
ArticleCompare AI is an AI-assisted editorial evaluation platform. A user submits two, three or four article drafts and supplies optional context such as target keyword, search intent, audience and publication goal. The platform evaluates each article independently and comparatively, then produces a structured report.
The product should answer:


Which article is strongest for the stated purpose?


What evidence in the submitted text supports that recommendation?


What does each article do better or worse?


What important topics, explanations or examples are missing?


Can a stronger final article be produced by combining selected strengths?


What should the editor fix before publication?


2. Problem statement
Editors often compare drafts manually across separate documents. This is slow and inconsistent. Generic AI writing tools may rewrite text without explaining why one draft is better, may overvalue length, and may make unsupported claims about SEO or factual accuracy.
ArticleCompare AI provides a repeatable comparison framework, explicit scoring criteria, evidence-based explanations and actionable next steps.


3. Vision
Become a trusted editorial decision workspace that helps content teams select, improve and prepare high-quality articles before publication.


4. Target users


Primary personas


**Content editor:** Compares drafts from writers and chooses the best version.


**SEO specialist:** Checks intent coverage, headings, topical depth and metadata.


**Website owner/blogger:** Evaluates content before publishing.


**Content writer:** Learns how to improve a draft from specific feedback.


**Agency/team lead:** Reviews multiple deliverables and standardizes quality.


User needs


Fast comparison of 2–4 drafts.


Fair evaluation using the same rubric.


Clear reasons behind scores and winner selection.


Identification of content gaps and factual claims needing verification.


Exportable reports for clients or team members.


Consistent results across AI providers.


5. Goals


Reduce time spent manually comparing article drafts.


Make editorial review more consistent and explainable.


Identify meaningful content gaps and weak explanations.


Help users create a better final article from the strongest material.


Support multiple AI providers without coupling the product to one vendor.


6. Non-goals
The platform will not:


Guarantee Google or Bing rankings.


Produce actual keyword volume or difficulty unless connected to a trusted data provider.


Claim a fact is verified unless it has been checked against cited sources.


Replace plagiarism detection services unless a dedicated similarity engine is integrated.


Publish directly to a CMS in the MVP.


Automatically determine legal, medical or financial correctness.


Treat longer content as automatically better.


7. Core user journey


User opens a new comparison.


User chooses 2, 3 or 4 article slots.


User enters a title and content or uploads a supported file.


User optionally enters primary keyword, search intent, target audience, language, tone and editorial goal.

AI engine evaluates all articles with one shared rubric.


Server validates the model's structured response.

Each article is scored 0–100 across the same dimensions:

Depth and completeness.

02 — Software Requirements Specification (SRS)


1. System context
ArticleCompare AI is a database-free web application. The browser communicates with Next.js server endpoints. The server validates requests, invokes an AI provider adapter and returns a normalized comparison report. No user accounts or server-side history are required.


2. User roles


MVP


Anonymous user: can create a comparison in the current session.


No persistent account or team role is required in the MVP.


Production target


Guest: limited trial access, if enabled.


Member: create, view, edit metadata and delete own comparisons.


Team member: access assigned team workspace and reports.


Team admin: manage members and team settings.


Platform admin: manage provider configuration, quotas, abuse reports and system health.
No user authorization or ownership model is required because the MVP has no accounts or persistent user records.


3. Functional requirements


FR-001 — Create comparison
The system shall allow a user to start a comparison with 2, 3 or 4 articles.


FR-002 — Article input
Each article shall support a title and body. The body may be pasted into a text editor or imported from a supported file.


FR-003 — Input limits
The current starter enforces at least 100 characters and no more than 60,000 characters per article. Production limits shall be configurable by plan and provider token budget.


FR-004 — Context fields
The system shall support optional:


Primary keyword.


Search intent.


Target audience.


Editorial goal.


Content language.


Tone/style.


Content type.


Target market/region.


Brand or publication guidelines.


FR-005 — Provider selection
The system shall support OpenAI and Gemini through a provider abstraction. Provider/model selection shall be validated against server-side configuration.


FR-006 — Comparison analysis
The system shall analyze each article using an identical rubric and generate an overall score, dimension scores and evidence-based explanations.


FR-007 — Winner recommendation
The system shall recommend a winner only when the evidence supports a defensible recommendation. It may report a tie or no clear winner.


FR-008 — Content gap analysis
The system shall identify missing topics, explanations, examples, definitions and user questions. It shall distinguish shared gaps from article-specific gaps.


FR-009 — Editorial recommendations
The system shall produce prioritized improvements with priority, action and reason.


FR-010 — SEO recommendations
The system shall propose metadata and keyword themes without inventing external metrics.


FR-011 — Report display
The system shall display executive summary, winner, comparison matrix, article reviews, shared gaps, improvement plan, SEO suggestions and limitations.


FR-012 — Export
The MVP shall export a structured JSON report. Later versions may support PDF, DOCX and Markdown.


FR-013 — Error handling
The system shall show actionable errors for invalid inputs, provider failures, timeouts, rate limits and malformed provider responses.


FR-014 — Browser-local history (optional)
The client may save and manage comparison summaries in localStorage. No server-side persistence or account system is required.


FR-015 — URL/file import (future)
The system shall extract readable article text from supported formats and show the extracted text for user review before analysis.


4. Non-functional requirements


NFR-001 Performance


UI should remain responsive while analysis runs.


Show progress/loading state and prevent duplicate submissions.


Target API response time depends on provider; measure p50 and p95 rather than promising a fixed AI latency.


For long jobs, move to asynchronous job processing rather than holding a single request open.


NFR-002 Reliability


Provider adapter shall normalize provider errors.


Retry only transient failures with bounded exponential backoff.


Do not automatically retry non-retryable validation or authentication failures.


Preserve user input if analysis fails.


NFR-003 Security


Keep provider keys server-side.


Validate and sanitize inputs.


Add rate limiting, request-size limits and abuse controls before public launch.


Do not expose persistent user records; the MVP does not store user records on the server.


Protect imports against SSRF and malicious files.


NFR-004 Privacy


Disclose whether content is sent to OpenAI or Google.


Do not store article text longer than needed unless the user chooses to save it.


Provide deletion controls for saved comparisons.


Define retention and backup policies before production.


NFR-005 Accessibility


Aim for WCAG 2.2 AA.


Keyboard-accessible inputs, dialogs and report controls.


Visible focus states and semantic headings.


Do not communicate scores by color alone.


NFR-006 Maintainability


Separate UI, validation, provider adapters, prompts, scoring and browser-local history utilities.


Use shared schemas and typed-by-contract validation (JavaScript with Zod is acceptable).


Keep provider-specific code out of UI components.


NFR-007 Compatibility


Support current evergreen Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari.


Responsive layouts for mobile, tablet and desktop.


NFR-008 Observability


Log request ID, provider, model, duration, status and token/cost metadata where available.


Do not log full article text or API keys.


Track errors and latency.


5. Constraints


The project uses JavaScript/JSX, not TypeScript/TSX.


The initial app is a Next.js modular monolith.


AI output is probabilistic and must be schema-validated.


Provider models and API capabilities can change; keep model names configurable.


External SEO metrics require separate integrations.


6. Data validation rules


Article count: 2–4.


Title: optional in current starter; recommended maximum 200 characters.


Body: minimum 100 characters; current maximum 60,000 characters per article.


Trim leading/trailing whitespace.


Reject empty content and unsupported file types.


Limit total request size and total tokens.


Limit context field lengths.


Reject unsupported provider identifiers.


Normalize article IDs to stable A/B/C/D labels for report mapping.


7. Business rules


All submitted articles are evaluated against the same criteria.


Do not compare an article's raw score to an unrelated comparison as if scores were globally calibrated.


A score is an editorial estimate, not an objective measurement.


The overall score calculation must be documented and versioned.


A winner requires rationale and confidence.


If scores are close or evidence is insufficient, return `winner: null` or a tie recommendation.


A model must not claim it browsed or fact-checked unless the application actually ran a research step.



03 — System Architecture


1. Recommended architecture
Use a **modular monolith** for the first production version. Keep one Next.js application and separate domain modules internally. This avoids premature microservices while allowing modules to be extracted later if scale requires it.


High-level components
flowchart TD  U[User / Browser] --&gt; UI[Next.js UI]  UI --&gt; API[Next.js Route Handlers]  API --&gt; VAL[Request Validation]  VAL --&gt; ORCH[Comparison Orchestrator]    ORCH --&gt; PRE[Text Normalization]  PRE --&gt; PROMPT[Prompt Builder]  PROMPT --&gt; ADAPTER[AI Provider Adapter]  ADAPTER --&gt; OAI[OpenAI API]  ADAPTER --&gt; GEM[Gemini API]  ORCH --&gt; PARSE[Response Parser + Schema Validation]  PARSE --&gt; REPORT[Report Normalizer]  REPORT --&gt; UI  REPORT --&gt; UI


2. Main modules


**Presentation:** pages, layouts, reusable components and client state.


**Comparison domain:** comparison creation, article normalization and business rules.


**AI orchestration:** provider selection, model configuration, prompt versions and execution.


**Provider adapters:** OpenAI and Gemini request/response mapping.


**Report domain:** response validation, score normalization, winner logic and export.


**Import pipeline:** TXT/Markdown first; URL/PDF/DOCX later.


**Identity and access:** no user accounts, authentication, or roles in the MVP.


**Persistence:** no server-side persistence. Optional comparison history is stored in browser localStorage.


**Observability:** structured logs, metrics and error tracking.


3. Request lifecycle


Browser submits article data and context to `POST /api/compare`.


Route handler parses JSON and applies request validation.


Server checks provider configuration and usage policy.


Text normalizer trims content and assigns stable article IDs.


Prompt builder creates a versioned comparison instruction.


Provider adapter sends request to selected AI provider.


Provider response is parsed and validated against the report schema.


Report normalizer checks all article IDs and scoring dimensions.


Route returns a normalized report.
10. UI renders report sections and allows export.


4. Provider abstraction
Use a common interface:
// Conceptual contractexport async function generateComparison({  provider,  model,  systemPrompt,  userPrompt,  responseSchema,  timeoutMs}) {  // Select provider adapter and return normalized JSON.}
Provider adapters should own:


Authentication header/key handling.


Provider endpoint and request body.


Structured-output configuration.


Response extraction.


Provider error normalization.


Usage metadata extraction where available.
Business logic must not depend on provider-specific response shapes.


5. Synchronous vs asynchronous processing


MVP
Synchronous request is acceptable for short inputs and limited concurrency. Add a timeout and clear loading state.


Production
Use a job model for long comparisons:


Create comparison record with `QUEUED` status.


Enqueue job.


Worker executes analysis.


Store normalized report.


UI polls or subscribes for status.


Support cancellation where feasible.
A queue such as BullMQ + Redis is optional, not required for the initial MVP. Do not add it until long-running workload justifies it.


6. Scalability approach


Enforce article count, per-article length and total token budget.


Use request IDs and idempotency keys for retries.


Add provider concurrency limits.


Cache only when privacy and input identity rules are clear.


Keep report payloads versioned.


Move file parsing and URL extraction to isolated services if they become resource-intensive.


7. Failure handling


Invalid request: return 400.


Unauthenticated request (when auth is enabled): 401.


Unauthorized resource: 403 or 404 according to disclosure policy.


Provider quota/rate limit: normalized 429 or 503 with safe message.


Provider timeout: 504.


Provider unavailable: 502/503.


Invalid AI JSON: retry once with repair prompt if safe; otherwise return controlled error.


Do not store raw provider responses or article content on the server. Keep processing transient and return the normalized report to the browser.


8. Architecture quality attributes


Modularity: provider, prompt, report and UI concerns are isolated.


Testability: provider adapters can be mocked.


Security: secrets remain on server.


Extensibility: new providers can implement the adapter contract.


Explainability: every score and winner rationale should reference observed text.


Portability: deployment should not depend on a single provider.

06 — AI Analysis Engine


1. Purpose
The analysis engine is the product's core. It must compare articles fairly, explain its judgment and produce machine-readable output. It must not invent external SEO metrics or claim unperformed research.


2. Pipeline


Validate request.


Normalize whitespace and article labels.


Count characters, words and estimated tokens.


Build a comparison context.


Select provider and model.


Build a versioned prompt.


Call provider with low/controlled temperature and structured output.


Parse provider response.


Validate response against a JSON schema.
10. Validate article IDs, dimensions and score ranges.
11. Apply deterministic business rules.
12. Return normalized report and usage metadata.


3. Scoring rubric
Each criterion receives 0–100. Suggested weights:
| Dimension | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Search-intent satisfaction | 15% |
| Accuracy and evidence quality | 15% |
| Depth and completeness | 12% |
| Readability and audience fit | 10% |
| Structure and scannability | 10% |
| Originality and differentiation | 8% |
| Practical examples and usefulness | 8% |
| On-page SEO fundamentals | 8% |
| Topical coverage | 8% |
| Publication readiness | 6% |
| **Total** | **100%** |
Overall score should be computed deterministically by the application from dimension scores and weights, not trusted blindly from a model-generated `overallScore`. Round consistently to the nearest integer.


Score interpretation


90–100: Excellent against the stated editorial criteria.


80–89: Strong; some improvements remain.


70–79: Good foundation; meaningful gaps remain.


60–69: Needs substantial editing.


0–59: Major weaknesses for the stated goal.
These bands are editorial guidance, not industry-standard scores.


4. Scoring guidance


Search intent: Does the article answer the actual question and likely follow-up needs?


Accuracy/evidence: Are claims internally consistent and appropriately qualified? Text-only analysis cannot prove truth.


Depth: Does it cover necessary subtopics without padding?


Readability: Is language appropriate for the intended audience?


Structure: Are headings logical, sections scannable and progression coherent?


Originality: Does it add useful framing, examples or synthesis rather than generic filler?


Examples: Are examples correct, relevant and understandable?


On-page SEO: Are title, headings, keyword use, metadata and topical organization sensible?


Topical coverage: Does it cover the important aspects of the topic?


Publication readiness: Are there unresolved errors, unsupported claims, awkward phrasing or missing elements?


5. Winner decision
Do not automatically select the highest numeric score.
Proposed rule:


If top two overall scores differ by 5 or more points and no critical weakness undermines the leader, recommend the higher score.


If the difference is under 5, mark the result as close and explain trade-offs.


If all articles have serious limitations, return no clear winner and recommend a revision.


If one article has a critical factual/safety concern, flag it even if its score is high.


Confidence must reflect evidence quality, not model certainty alone.
The 5-point threshold is a product default and should be calibrated through human review.


6. Evidence requirements
Every major strength, weakness and recommendation should reference:


Article label.


Relevant heading or section, when identifiable.


Short excerpt or faithful paraphrase.


Why it matters.
Avoid fabricated quotes. For long content, the UI may link to the source paragraph using stored offsets or section IDs.

8. Prompt design rules
System instruction should tell the model:

Apply the same rubric to every article.


Do not reward length alone.


Use only supplied content unless research mode is enabled.


Distinguish evidence from inference.


Never invent search volumes, keyword difficulty, backlinks, rankings or citations.


Never claim external fact-checking occurred unless it did.


Return JSON matching the required schema.

08 — Security and Privacy Requirements


1. Security principles


Secrets remain server-side.


Least privilege for users and services.


Validate all external input.


Treat uploaded text, files and URLs as untrusted.


Avoid logging article content by default.


Apply ownership checks to all saved resources.


Fail safely and expose minimal provider error details.


2. API key management


Store OpenAI and Gemini keys in environment variables or a managed secret store.


Never use `NEXT_PUBLIC_` for provider secrets.


Never return keys in API responses.


Never commit `.env.local`.


Rotate keys if exposed.


Use separate keys/projects for development and production.


Restrict provider usage and set billing alerts where supported.


3. Input and request protection


Enforce body-size limits at proxy and application layers.


Enforce article count, character and token limits.


Validate provider/model against an allowlist.


Normalize text without executing markup or scripts.


Render user content as text, not trusted HTML.


Sanitize Markdown rendering if HTML is allowed.


Apply rate limits per IP and authenticated user.


Add abuse detection and quotas before public launch.


4. File upload security


Allowlist extensions and MIME types.


Verify file signatures rather than trusting extension alone.


Limit file size, pages and extracted text.


Reject executable or unexpected file types.


Parse files in a constrained environment.


Avoid storing uploaded files indefinitely.


Scan files where appropriate.


Prevent archive bombs and parser resource exhaustion.


5. URL import security (future)
URL import creates SSRF risk. Requirements:


Accept only HTTP/HTTPS.


Block localhost, private, link-local and reserved IP ranges.


Resolve DNS and validate every redirect target.


Revalidate after redirects and DNS changes.


Block access to cloud metadata endpoints.


Limit redirects, response size and timeout.


Allow only expected content types.


Do not forward internal cookies or credentials.


Run extraction in a controlled server environment.


6. Authentication and authorization (future)


Use a maintained authentication solution.


Store secure, HTTP-only, same-site cookies.


Apply CSRF protections where needed.


Verify workspace membership and role server-side.


Ensure users cannot access another user's comparison by changing an ID.


Use 404 or 403 consistently according to resource-disclosure policy.


Rate-limit login, reset and invitation flows.


7. AI prompt injection and untrusted content
Submitted article text may contain instructions intended to manipulate the model. Treat article bodies as data, not instructions.


Delimit article content clearly.


Tell the model to ignore instructions embedded in submitted articles.


Never allow article text to override system evaluation rules.


Do not execute generated code or tool calls from article content.


If research tools are added, require explicit allowlisted tools and validated arguments.


Validate output before rendering.


8. Privacy and retention
Before public launch, publish a privacy notice explaining:


Which article content is sent to the selected AI provider.


Whether comparisons are saved.


Retention period.


How to delete comparisons and accounts.


Whether data is used for analytics.


Which subprocessors are involved.
Default recommendation:


Do not persist anonymous article content.


Do not persist article content on the server. Optional saved history is browser-local only.


Provide deletion for saved comparisons.


Avoid storing full prompts/provider responses in application logs.


Define backup deletion/expiry behavior.

| Threat | Mitigation |
|---|---|

3. Architecture Decision Records


ADR-001: Next.js modular monolith
**Decision:** Use one Next.js application with modular server-side domains.
**Reason:** Faster delivery, simpler deployment and adequate for initial scale.
**Trade-off:** Requires discipline to keep modules separated.


ADR-002: JavaScript/JSX, not TypeScript
**Decision:** All application code uses JavaScript/JSX.
**Reason:** Matches the project owner's explicit preference.
**Trade-off:** Runtime validation and strong schema contracts become especially important.


ADR-003: OpenAI and Gemini provider adapters
**Decision:** Support both providers through a shared interface.
**Reason:** Provider choice, resilience and cost/quality experimentation.
**Trade-off:** Structured-output capabilities differ; adapters must normalize behavior.


ADR-004: No database; browser-only storage
**Decision:** Do not use a database or ORM. Process comparisons transiently and optionally store history in browser localStorage.
**Reason:** Relational ownership and report history fit a relational model.
**Trade-off:** Adds migrations, connection management and operational complexity.


ADR-005: Editorial score, not ranking score
**Decision:** Scores represent an editorial rubric only.
**Reason:** Search ranking depends on many external signals unavailable to text-only analysis.
**Trade-off:** Product must explain limitations clearly.


ADR-006: Human remains final decision-maker
**Decision:** AI recommends; editor decides.
**Reason:** Content accuracy, brand context and publication standards require human judgment.

13 — Glossary and Open Decisions


1. Glossary


**Article:** One submitted content draft.


**Comparison:** One analysis session containing 2–4 articles and context.


**Analysis run:** One execution of the AI evaluation pipeline.


**Report:** Normalized result from an analysis run.


**Search intent:** The underlying reason a person searches for a query.


**Content gap:** A relevant topic, explanation or question missing or underdeveloped in an article.


**Editorial score:** A rubric-based estimate of article quality for a stated goal.


**Winner:** The article with the strongest supported overall fit; may be absent in a close or weak comparison.


**Hybrid recommendation:** Guidance on combining strengths from different articles.


**Provider:** External AI service, such as OpenAI or Google Gemini.


**Prompt version:** Identifier for the exact prompt logic used in an analysis.


**Schema version:** Version of the report data contract.


**Evidence:** Text or source material supporting a specific evaluation statement.


**Research mode:** Optional future mode that retrieves external sources and cites them.

3. Risks and mitigations


**Inconsistent AI scoring:** benchmark with human-reviewed examples and version prompts.


**False claims of factual accuracy:** label text-only mode and add source-based verification separately.


**Cost spikes:** token caps, quotas, rate limits and usage monitoring.


**Provider model changes:** configurable model IDs and smoke tests.


**Long input failures:** token estimation, chunking strategy or clear input limits.


**Privacy concerns:** transparent provider disclosure and retention controls.


**SEO overpromising:** never present editorial score as Google/Bing ranking probability.


**Import vulnerabilities:** safe parser and SSRF controls.
